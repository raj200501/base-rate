# Related work notes

These notes position base-rate against the references in section 2 of docs/BUILD_BRIEF.md. The gap base-rate addresses is statistically valid evaluation of agent monitors at deployment prevalence and fixed human-review budgets, with certified intervals, sample-size calculators, threshold transfer checks, and cost-optimal cascades.

Only references marked verified in artifacts/citecheck.json are described. Findings are described qualitatively on purpose, so take any result numbers from the sources and not from this file. Reading depth differs by source. The Practical challenges paper, the LinuxArena post, and the SHADE-Arena post were read as full text. The Async Control paper and BashArena were read by abstract plus targeted passages on metrics, intervals, and deployment models. MonitorBench and TRACE were read from their abstract pages only, and Armour was read through a fetch tool summary of its public README.

## practical-challenges-2025: Practical challenges of control monitoring in frontier AI deployments

Lindner, Griffin, Korbak, Zimmermann, Irving, Farquhar, and Cooney write a conceptual analysis of what changes when control monitoring moves from simplified evaluations to real deployments. They name four dynamics that earlier work set aside: many agent instances running in parallel, oversight that takes time, attacks that make partial progress across instances that share an environment, and the fact that one suspicious action may not justify shutting an agent down. They compare fully synchronous, semi-synchronous, and asynchronous monitoring, and they give a small availability model based on the flag rate, the audit duration, and the number of instances. They add recovery, meaning harm prevention and reversion to a safe state, as a complement to shutdown, and they sketch a safety case from six claims about monitorability, oversight latency, harm latency, harm prevention, and reversibility. Four case studies apply the sketch to weight exfiltration, training data poisoning, research sabotage, and secret exfiltration, and the authors say these are hypothetical and need empirical validation before use in any real deployment. On statistics, the paper says monitor recall, human recall, and latencies can be estimated with control evaluations and extrapolated conservatively, but it gives no estimator, interval, or sample size for any of them. Its clearest link to review capacity is an illustration that if auditors can review only a small share of traffic, the monitor must be calibrated to the matching false positive rate, and recall at that rate then drives safety. Its appendix models lower-bound the chance of catching and recovering from an attack by multiplying per-claim probabilities under independence, and the text warns that those independence assumptions are hard to justify. A text search of the full paper finds no mention of prevalence, precision, confidence intervals, sample sizes, review budgets, or cascades of monitors.

base-rate does not compete with the paper's protocol analysis, and it supplies the measurement layer that the safety case needs as input. Where the paper says monitor recall at a given false positive rate can be estimated by a control evaluation, base-rate states how many labeled positives and negatives that estimate needs for a given confidence, and which interval to report. Where the paper fixes review capacity as a false positive rate, base-rate evaluates recall and precision under a fixed number of human reviews per unit of traffic at stated deployment prevalences. Precision depends on prevalence even when recall at a fixed false positive rate does not, and the paper does not discuss precision at all. Where the paper assumes thresholds are calibrated to the review capacity, base-rate tests whether a threshold tuned in one domain or at one prevalence still meets its false positive budget in another. Where the paper treats the monitor as one component with a recall figure, base-rate studies ensembles and cascades and reports the cost and recall tradeoff under the review budget. The step-level labels and time-to-detection measure in base-rate give an empirical handle on the monitor stage of the paper's oversight latency claim. The paper notes that monitor scores within one context may be correlated, so base-rate should resample whole trajectories and not single steps when it computes intervals. base-rate does not address synchrony, cross-instance coordination, recovery, or shutdown policy, and its results are best read as inputs to the per-attempt catch rate in the paper's safety case and not as a replacement for that case.

## aisi-async-control-2025: Async Control (UK AI Security Institute)

The paper studies asynchronous monitoring of software engineering agents, where a monitor reviews actions after the fact instead of blocking them, and it frames monitor development as a game between a blue team that builds monitors and a red team that builds sabotaging agents. The main metric is the false negative rate of the monitor at a fixed low false positive rate, measured on benign and attack episodes generated for the game, with a held-out environment used for testing. It then extrapolates from the measured false negative rate to deployment risk with a simple queueing model that includes an audit rate, the delay before an attack causes harm, and the number of successful attacks needed for harm, and it lists the assumptions the model needs, such as perfect auditing and independent detection. Error bars come from bootstrapping over the generated episodes, and because the episodes are produced for the game, the share of attacks in the evaluation data is a design choice and not a deployment prevalence. The paper does report monitor behavior on out-of-distribution settings, but it does not report a procedure for deciding whether a threshold calibrated in one setting still meets its false positive budget in another, and its text has no sample-size calculator. It limits monitor inference cost to the cost of running the untrusted agent, but it does not frame the choice as a cost and recall front over cascades.

## basharena-2025: BashArena

BashArena is a control setting of Linux system administration and infrastructure tasks in which a red team agent tries to finish a hidden sabotage objective, such as running malware, leaking secrets, escalating privileges, or disabling a firewall, while a monitor tries to flag it. The authors measure how often attackers of different strength succeed without being flagged when the monitor runs at a fixed trajectory-level false positive rate, and they release the dataset and the task generation pipeline. Intervals on safety come from bootstrapping clean and attack trajectories together. It is an attacker-versus-monitor benchmark, so attack trajectories are produced by the red team and scored alongside clean ones, and it says nothing about monitor behavior at deployment prevalence. For base-rate it is a source of realistic attack data and violation types, but its evaluation unit is the whole trajectory, whereas base-rate needs step-level labels and a much larger pool of benign trajectories.

## linuxarena-2026: Introducing LinuxArena (Redwood Research blog)

This blog post announces LinuxArena, a control setting made of software engineering environments, each with main tasks, possible safety failures, and covert sabotage trajectories that cause those failures. It describes three uses: sabotage evaluations of attacker models, monitor evaluations that need an honest dataset and an attack dataset, and control evaluations of protocols such as trusted monitoring and defer to trusted. Monitor results are shown as safety at a fixed action-level false positive rate against attackers of increasing strength, including human-written attacks that the monitor largely misses. The post is an announcement and not a statistical method, and in its text it does not discuss prevalence, intervals, sample sizes, or review budgets beyond the choice of the false positive rate. For base-rate, LinuxArena is a possible source of labeled attack trajectories and a possible target for the plug-in evaluation, subject to its access terms, which have not been checked.

## shade-arena-2025: SHADE-Arena (Anthropic)

The post describes SHADE-Arena, a set of agentic environments in which a model does a benign main task while secretly pursuing a harmful side task, and a second model reads the transcript and rates how suspicious it is. A run counts as successful sabotage only if both tasks are completed and the suspicion score stays under a set threshold, and the post studies how giving the monitor access to the agent's reasoning changes the outcome. It reports that the best monitors tested were not good enough for deployment, because catching most side tasks cost a false positive rate too high to be practical, and that the best monitors did better than human readers on the same transcripts. The post uses the false positive cost as a feasibility argument, and base-rate turns that intuition into computed precision and reviewer load at stated prevalence and review budgets. The post does not address threshold transfer or intervals that account for rare positives.

## monitorbench-2026: MonitorBench (chain-of-thought monitorability)

MonitorBench is a benchmark for chain-of-thought monitorability, meaning whether a model's visible reasoning reveals the factors that actually drive its behavior, so that a monitor reading the reasoning can use them. It provides test instances with defined decision-critical factors across many tasks and categories, plus two stress-test prompting settings that try to lower monitorability, and it varies both the evaluated model and the monitor model. The abstract reports that monitorability depends on the evaluated model, the monitor model, and the task, and that many models can reduce it when prompted to, mainly on some task types. It measures whether a monitor can detect a reasoning factor, and not how often real policy violations occur in agent traffic, so it does not address prevalence or review budgets. For base-rate it is context for one monitor input channel, the reasoning trace, and a reminder that monitor quality depends on what the monitor can see.

## trace-2026: TRACE (reward hack detection in code environments)

The paper introduces TRACE, a benchmark of coding trajectories containing reward hacks across a large taxonomy of exploit categories, curated synthetically and checked by humans, for testing whether a language model judge can spot reward hacking. Its central comparison is between an isolated classification setup, where the judge sees one trajectory, and a contrastive setup, where the judge sees a group of trajectories and picks out the anomalous ones, and detection is better in the contrastive setup. It also reports that the ratio of benign to hacked trajectories and the size of the analysis cluster affect detection performance, which is the closest the abstract comes to prevalence. The ratios studied are benchmark mixing choices at small scale and not deployment prevalences, and the abstract does not describe interval estimates or review budgets. For base-rate, TRACE is a related labeled source for code environments, although reward hacking is not one of the violation types in the brief's taxonomy, and it is independent evidence that class mix changes measured detection, which supports reporting results at fixed prevalence.

## armour: Armour (Raj's repository)

Armour is an MIT-licensed, dependency-light evaluation framework for trace-level safety in tool-using agents, and it checks the sequence of tool calls and arguments and whether the agent reported its actions accurately, not only the final answer. It provides a normalized trace schema, a set of built-in cases across workflows such as software, customer data, finance, HR, procurement, browser use, and eval integrity, rule monitors for misuse, data overreach, credential access, false completion, policy bypass, and audit tampering, and policy packs that can be replayed over recorded traces. It includes a small judge comparison between a reviewer-calibrated monitor and a generic LLM-judge proxy, and its README, as summarized by the fetch tool, describes the sample-size caveats and calls its own claims narrow. As summarized, that comparison reports raw counts on a small sample and does not claim a prevalence-level estimate, which is the gap base-rate fills. base-rate imports or re-implements Armour's trace schema and uses its rule monitors and taxonomy as the baseline monitor family. The brief also lists a prevalence report command for Armour, prepared as a patch, as a stretch item.

## hua-cascades-2025: Combining cost-constrained runtime monitors for AI safety

Hua, Baskerville, Lemoine, Hopman, Bhatt, and Tracy ask how to combine several runtime monitors, such as prompted language-model monitors of different size and cost, into one protocol that catches as many misaligned outputs as possible within a single budget that covers both monitor calls and audits. They frame the protocol as a decision problem, use the Neyman-Pearson lemma to rank outputs by likelihood ratio, estimate those ratios with kernel density estimation, and compare policy classes that include randomized monitor calling and calling a second monitor only inside a score range. It is evaluated on backdoored code in a public coding benchmark, with recall on held-out misaligned samples as the main metric and cost measured on benign outputs. Uncertainty comes from repeated random train and test splits of the misaligned data, shown as quantile intervals, with paired comparisons between policies and the benign set treated as fixed. The paper notes that misaligned outputs are rare and that interventions should touch only a small share of benign outputs, so the benign tail matters, and it admits that the tail is sparsely sampled and the misaligned test set is small. Its limits, in its own words, include reliance on the attack distribution matching the training distribution, possible overfitting of the fitted decision regions, exhaustive search that does not scale past a few monitors, and costs that are illustrative. base-rate asks a neighboring cascade question, and this paper is the closest prior art for it. The difference is the evidence layer: base-rate fixes a deployment prevalence and a human-review budget, reports recall and precision with valid confidence intervals, sizes the labeled samples needed to certify a claim, and checks whether a threshold transfers across domains, none of which this paper treats as a goal. base-rate also treats the cascade as an optimization under a review budget with certified intervals on its operating point, and does not rely on a density-based policy search over a single benchmark. The two are complementary, since a policy found by their method could be passed to base-rate for certification at stated prevalences.

## korbak-safety-case-2025: A sketch of an AI control safety case

Korbak, Clymer, Hilton, Shlegeris, and Irving propose a control safety case, a structured argument that a model cannot get around safeguards such as monitoring to cause unacceptable outcomes. As a case study they sketch why a hypothetical internally deployed agent would not leak sensitive data, using evidence from a red-team exercise in a proxy environment. The argument depends on three assumptions that the authors state: the red team fully elicits the model's abilities, the controls work as well in real deployment, and performance is extrapolated conservatively. The conservative extrapolation step is where base-rate fits, because it needs monitor recall and precision estimates with valid intervals at realistic prevalence, which the sketch does not supply. base-rate offers measurement tooling for that step and does not attempt to build or assess a safety case.

A review on 2026-11-04 questioned whether the README's summary of these two papers goes
beyond what the notes above record (the Korbak sketch had been read through its abstract

https://arxiv.org/html/2501.17315 and https://arxiv.org/html/2512.22154) and the
passages were checked in their saved copies:

- korbak-safety-case-2025: "The safety layer edits the 10% most suspicious actions and
  audits the 0.1% most suspicious actions"; the blue team keeps "the action edits and
  audits within predetermined budgets"; "human review requirements present the primary
  scaling constraint"; and claim C12.1, "developers conservatively extrapolate statistics
  from the proxy deployment to estimate the probability of data exfiltration". The text
  has no interval, sample-size calculation, or estimator for recall or the false-positive
  rate beyond measuring how reliably the monitors flag attacks.
- practical-challenges-2025: "First we need to estimate statistics about our various
  claims, such as monitor and human recall" and "we need to find a way to conservatively
  extrapolate them".

The README's wording (review capacity fixes the false-positive rate, recall then drives
safety, and the quantities must be estimated and extrapolated conservatively, with no
estimator, interval, or sample size given) is supported by these passages.
